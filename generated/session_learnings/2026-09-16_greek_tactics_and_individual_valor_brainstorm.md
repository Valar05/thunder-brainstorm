# Greek Tactics + Individual Valor Brainstorm

Date: 2026-09-16
Source type: historical military patterns transformed into generalized game mechanics
Status: brainstorm material, not canon

## Design target

Greek warfare should feel like a contest of **formation geometry, cohesion, terrain, timing, and civic obligation**. The hoplite phalanx is not a magic wall. Its strength comes from keeping a collective shape under pressure, while light troops, cavalry, terrain, and commanders determine whether that shape reaches the right place at the right time.

The individual is powerful primarily through **discipline, position, example, and local decision**.

## Tactical pattern vocabulary

### Hoplite cohesion

A dense shield-bearing formation depends on men maintaining alignment and mutual protection. The useful game abstraction is not an exact historical shove simulation, but **cohesion as a resource**.

**Mechanic:** every formation has frontage, depth, cohesion, and orientation. Abrupt turns, gaps, casualties, rough terrain, pursuit, and mixed-speed movement erode cohesion.

A formation with high cohesion can advance, absorb contact, and deliver a concentrated push. A formation with low cohesion must reform before it can perform reliably.

### The dangerous rightward drift

Greek hoplite formations could exhibit a tendency for men to seek the protection of the shield-bearing neighbor on their right, creating tactical drift. Treat this as a readable emergent tendency rather than a universal law.

**Mechanic:** under fear and contact pressure, formation alignment gradually shifts toward the protected side. Commanders can compensate, exploit the opponent's drift, or deliberately create an angled engagement.

This makes seemingly simple straight-line infantry produce geometry that players must anticipate.

### Oblique attack / concentrated wing

At Leuctra in 371 BCE, Epaminondas used an unusually deep concentration of troops on his left wing while holding other elements back, producing an oblique attack. Sources differ on how much of the technique should be considered wholly novel, but the operational pattern is clear: **do not fight the whole enemy line on equal terms if you can make one part of it collapse first.** citeturn496953search0turn496953search2

**Mechanic:** formations may refuse one wing, delay contact, and mass a striking column against a chosen section.

### Refused flank

A weaker wing does not have to win. It can withdraw, delay, anchor on terrain, or simply avoid decisive contact while the stronger wing attacks elsewhere.

**Mechanic:** give the player explicit **delay / refuse / engage** stances. A refused flank buys time but cedes ground.

### Terrain as formation killer

Hoplite formations are most legible on suitable ground. Narrow lanes, broken slopes, gullies, walls, and obstacles can fragment a line and alter frontage.

**Mechanic:** terrain modifies maximum useful formation width and the speed at which cohesion decays. A commander can choose terrain partly to determine whose formation becomes awkward first.

### Light troops as disruption

Javelin troops, archers, slingers, and other lighter forces should not just be “weak infantry.” Their strategic function is often to harass, screen, pursue exposed units, and force heavy infantry to make inconvenient decisions.

**Mechanic:** light troops generate **disruption tokens**. Heavy formations can ignore them briefly, but doing so accumulates fatigue, broken order, or delayed responses.

### Cavalry and the exposed edge

Greek heavy infantry is vulnerable when its flank or rear is exposed. Cavalry therefore becomes dangerous through **access**, not raw damage.

**Mechanic:** cavalry cannot simply attack a stable frontal phalanx effectively. It wants a broken line, open side, retreat route, or terrain transition.

### Citizen army logistics

The social structure matters mechanically: troops have different incentives, endurance, and willingness to continue after the battlefield stops looking favorable.

**Mechanic:** morale is not one number. Track **cohesion, willingness, and civic obligation** separately. A formation might remain tactically coherent while deciding it has suffered enough and beginning an organized withdrawal.

## Individual valor patterns

### Holding the place in the line

The most basic Greek valor is not an individual duel. It is maintaining position while the formation around you is under pressure.

**Game version:** a named hoplite can choose to maintain rank after losing health, equipment, or a neighbor. Doing so prevents a local cohesion collapse but raises injury risk.

### Step into the gap

When a neighbor falls or the formation opens, an individual can move laterally or forward to restore contact.

**Mechanic:** **gap-closing reaction**. A nearby character may spend personal safety to restore a formation edge before enemies enter it.

### Runner / messenger under fire

A command plan can fail because orders do not arrive. A volunteer runner can restore coordination at serious risk.

**Game version:** an individual carries an order token across a dangerous space. Success changes another unit's stance; failure delays or distorts the command.

### Shielding the wounded

A character can stop fighting long enough to protect a fallen comrade while the formation reorganizes.

**Mechanic:** a rescue action trades offensive capability for preservation of personnel and morale.

### Sacred Band / elite cohesion

The Sacred Band of Thebes is especially useful as a game abstraction for a small elite unit whose strength comes from **internal trust and cohesion**, not merely higher statistics.

**Mechanic:** elite pairs or small cells gain bonuses when they remain mutually supported. Separating them imposes an emotional and tactical cost.

### Commander in the dangerous place

An admired commander can be physically present in the key formation, but doing so exposes the command structure to catastrophic local loss.

**Mechanic:** command presence provides strong local bonuses while increasing a **leader exposure** risk. The player chooses whether leadership should be distributed or concentrated.

### Last man on the retreat route

A named fighter can stay behind to buy time for citizens, wounded troops, standards, or supplies to escape.

**Mechanic:** each turn of delay creates measurable downstream survival. The player can see exactly what the sacrifice buys without guaranteeing survival.

## Greek encounter recipes

### Leuctra-style asymmetric engagement

One wing is massively reinforced, another is deliberately cautious. The objective is not to win everywhere, but to destroy a chosen enemy concentration before it can generate its own advantage. The historical Battle of Leuctra is the anchor for this asymmetric geometry. citeturn496953search0turn496953search2

**Player decisions:**

1. conceal the real striking wing
2. delay the weak wing
3. protect the striking wing from cavalry/disruption
4. commit the elite at the decisive point
5. exploit the enemy's collapse before the rest of the line closes

### Narrow pass

A superior army must enter a space where only part of its frontage can fight.

**Game grammar:** width is more important than total numbers. Reserve troops matter because the active frontage is constrained.

**Valor hook:** an individual can hold a critical step, gate, or bend long enough for relief to arrive.

### Broken pursuit

The enemy routs, and the player's formation faces a choice: maintain order or chase.

**Mechanic:** pursuit generates immediate rewards but steadily destroys cohesion. A disciplined commander can refuse the chase and preserve a stronger formation for the next encounter.

### City-edge battle

Combat near walls, orchards, workshops, and roads produces fragmented local fights.

**Mechanic:** formations become smaller units temporarily, increasing the importance of named individuals, messengers, and local leaders.

## Individual valor scoring

Do not count kills. Score valor by what the action preserves or enables:

- closes a dangerous gap
- keeps a formation coherent
- carries an order that changes the battle
- protects a retreat
- prevents a rout
- rescues an important person
- holds a choke point
- buys time for the main formation
- refuses a tempting but strategically bad pursuit

The heroic action can therefore be **restraint** as often as aggression.

## Three Greek clocks

1. **Formation clock:** how long the collective body can maintain useful geometry.
2. **Terrain clock:** how quickly movement, turning, and cohesion become harder in the chosen ground.
3. **Civic clock:** how long the soldiers are willing to continue before withdrawal becomes preferable to further loss.

Individual valor often consists of spending personal safety to preserve the formation clock long enough for the other two clocks to move into a favorable state.

## Prototype-sized version

One field. One ridge. One narrow pass. Hoplites, light troops, cavalry, and one commander. Three named soldiers.

The prototype succeeds when the player can visibly:

- preserve a phalanx under pressure
- use terrain to restrict frontage
- weaken an enemy with light troops
- mass one wing while refusing another
- exploit an exposed edge with cavalry
- have a named individual close a gap or carry a critical order
- suffer a real loss without the whole battle becoming scripted

The central feeling should be **collective strength made visible through individual discipline**.
