## Bimodal combat policy

Drew's remembered sparring policy was effectively bimodal:

```text
MODE A — MAXIMUM MEASURE
cautious long-range poke
keep the opponent outside
make entry expensive

MODE B — MAXIMUM COMMITMENT
overcommitted zornhau
collapse measure violently
accept contact risk
use size / momentum / commitment to force the exchange
```

The strategically important feature is the missing middle. Drew did not need a large repertoire if the two available actions forced radically different answers.

Working law:

> **Repertoire width need not equal decision-space width.**

A very small action set can create a large opponent-model burden when its actions occupy different tactical regimes.

## Matchup asymmetry

Kenneth is remembered as an adaptive policy: multiple styles, opponent-sensitive response, teacher-level diagnostic breadth.

David is remembered as a broadly skilled conventional policy.

Drew is remembered as a narrow extreme-state policy.

So a simple scalar ranking like `Kenneth > David > Drew` does not fully predict matchup behavior. Drew's limited repertoire could still create a pathological matchup for David because the answers required at maximum measure and maximum commitment were very different.

This is not evidence that Drew was technically superior. It is evidence that technical breadth and opponent decision cost are separate variables.
